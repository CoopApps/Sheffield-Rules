# Historiography: The Sheffield Origins Debate

## Overview

The role of Sheffield in the development of modern association football has been the subject of intense academic debate since the 1990s. This entry examines the competing interpretations of Sheffield's influence, the evidence presented by different schools of thought, and the methodological issues at the heart of this historiographical controversy.

---

## The Traditional Narrative: Public School Origins

### The Respectability Thesis (1900s-1970s)

**Context**: Early football historians sought to legitimize the professional game by emphasizing its "respectable" public school origins.

**Key Works**:
- **Charles Sutcliffe and Fred Hargreaves** (1928): *History of the Lancashire Football Association 1878-1928*
  - Explicit desire to counter image of footballers as "Northern Hordes" or "Muddied Oafs"
  - Stressed that "Mayors, judges and even monarchs" now patronized football
  - Professional soccer seeking respectability in amateur British sport environment

- **Geoffrey Green** (1953, 1961): *History of the Football Association* and *Association Football* (4 volumes with A.H. Fabian)
  - Placed FA origins firmly in public school tradition
  - Privately educated author aware of rugby union's greater authority in elite schools

- **Percy Young**: Works on Manchester United, Wolverhampton Wanderers, Sheffield football
  - Followed respectability narrative

**Social Context**:
- 1950s-1960s: Rugby union increasing hegemony in public/grammar schools
- 1968 Chester Report noted "image of soccer acquired by impact of professionalism" influenced headmasters
- Soccer seen as lower-status than rugby in elite education

### Eric Dunning's Civilising Process Theory (1963-present)

**First Modern Academic Article**: "History Today" (December 1963)

**Core Argument**:
- Applied Norbert Elias' "civilising process" to football development
- Roots of modern soccer traced to Eton College field game (1840s)
- "Bifurcation" of football into association and rugby codes result of "status rivalry"
- Eton (most prestigious public school) vs Rugby School (the "upstart")
- **Soccer, not rugby, had the higher social status historically**

**Current Status**:
> "The argument laid out in this 1963 article remains essentially unchanged today, despite its supporters being unable to furnish any substantive evidence to support the theory."

**Followers**: Graham Curry and others

## The Revisionist Challenge (1980s-present)

### Political and Social Context

**Thatcher Era Background**:
- Government stigmatization of all football supporters as hooligans
- Valley Parade and Hillsborough disasters
- Development of Football Supporters' Association
- Self-published fanzine movement

**When Saturday Comes** (pioneering fanzine):
- Illustration: player in early 20th-century kit with "The People" emblazoned on chest
- Slogan: "Reclaim the Game"
- Belief that soccer taken away from working-class roots by businessmen and administrators
- **"Authenticity" replaced "respectability" as aspiration**

### First Wave Academic Historians (Late 1970s)

**Tony Mason** (1981): *Association Football and English Society 1863-1915*
- Pointed to "infinite number of forms" of early football
- Challenged assumption that folk football had disappeared

**Richard Holt**:
> "We cannot simply assume that so long and well-established a folk game had simply disappeared"

### The Revisionist School: Harvey and Goulstone

**Key Figures**:
- **John Goulstone**: Published extensive research since 1970s on early cricket and football
- **Adrian Harvey**: *Football: The First Hundred Years* (2005)

**Core Claims**:

1. **Many games organized before FA formation (1863)**:
   - Pre-agreed rules with varying degrees of regulation
   - Welcome corrective to misunderstanding that violent folk football was "civilized" by public schools

2. **Harvey's "Football's Missing Link"** (1999):
   - "Popular football was governed by rules, achieving a high degree of organisation in certain regions"
   - "The influence exerted by codes emanating from the public schools is... exaggerated"
   - "An organised football culture would have emerged in the latter half of the nineteenth century even if there had been no public school model"
   - **Public schools played little or no role**
   - **Working- and middle-class men were authentic pioneers**

3. **Sheffield as Wellspring**:
   - Goulstone: "One could quite convincingly trace soccer's main ancestral line before 1867 back to Sheffield in 1857"
   - Harvey: Sheffield had "the most important football culture that existed in the entire world during the 1850s and 1860s"

**Recent Supporters**:
- Peter Swain
- Roy Hay
- Accepted as new paradigm by many soccer supporters interested in early history

### The Academic Debate (Past 15 Years)

**Vigorous debate in sports history journals**:
- "Revisionists" (led by Harvey)
- vs.
- Followers of Eric Dunning (most notably Graham Curry)

**Positive Result**:
> "Both sides in the debate have conducted a huge amount of primary research into football during the 1840 to 1880 period, uncovering much that is new and insightful, for which everyone with an interest in football history should be grateful."

## T. Collins' Critique of the Revisionist Thesis

### Source

**Author**: T. Collins

**Published**: *The International Journal of the History of Sport*

**Argument**: Despite extensive research, revisionist findings are "no less flawed" than Dunning's followers

### Three Methodological Errors (Common to Both Schools)

1. **Academic overestimation of importance of written rules**
2. **Ahistoric view of culture and continuity**
3. **Tendency to view the past through lens of the present**

## Sheffield's Actual Influence on the FA

### The Three Examples Examined

Harvey cites three examples to support claim that "Sheffield saved the FA":

#### 1. Sheffield FC Letter (30 November 1863)

**Claim**: Letter to newly formed FA showed Sheffield's influence

**Reality**: "No impact on the decisions taken by the FA"

#### 2. London vs Sheffield Match (1866)

**Claim**: Demonstrated Sheffield-London cooperation

**Reality**: Match played under **FA rules**, not Sheffield code

#### 3. William Chesterman at 1867 FA AGM

**Claim**: Sheffield's presence at meeting showed influence

**Reality**:
- FA voted **against all three** of Sheffield's proposed amendments
- Meeting voted in favor of "greater harmony between the rules of the FA and those used by Westminster and Charterhouse [public schools]"

### The Youdan Cup Speculation

**Harvey's Theory**: Success of Youdan Cup (3,000 watched 1867 final) may have inspired FA with vision of football's potential

**Problems**:
- No evidence presented to support this speculation
- **Youdan Cup lasted only one year** - "not the most auspicious example"
- Cromwell Cup (1868) attracted only 4 sides

### Alternative Explanation: Change in FA Leadership

**Unexplored by Revisionists**:
- FA had 4 secretaries in first 7 years - lacked direction
- **Charles Alcock's** growing influence had major impact

**The Sportsman** (reporting on 1867 FA AGM):
> Alcock told delegates "he considered a little more energy was required to establish the game on a sure footing towards which its popularity was by degrees approaching"

**Alcock's Proposal**: Invite leading public schools and universities to join

**1868**: Appointed FA secretary
- Oversaw introduction of FA Cup
- Oversaw international matches

### Sheffield FA Joins the FA (1877)

**Critical Evidence**:
- Sheffield FA accepted **completely** the FA's rule book
- Accepted FA's stringent offside rule
- Abandoned Sheffield's "kick-in" for FA's throw-in
- Acknowledged by everyone at the time

**Sheffield & Rotherham Independent**:
> "These proposals [to adopt the FA rules] are made with a view to bringing the Sheffield rules in harmony with those played by the London and Scottish associations, and thus obtain a universal code for the dribbling game throughout the country."

**Conclusion**: Sheffield had "negligible influence on the FA itself"

## Detailed Analysis of Sheffield Rules

### The Three 1867 Proposed Rule Changes

Sheffield FC proposed to FA's 1867 general meeting:

1. **Free kick against holding, passing, or knocking on the ball**
   - Points toward modern soccer

2. **Offside called against any player between goal and goalkeeper**
   - Would mean **effectively no offside rule**
   - Game similar to Australian Rules Football

3. **Introduction of Eton's "rouge" scoring system**
   - Type of "minor point" counted only if teams level on goals

### Problem: The "Long-Kicking" Style

**Archibald Rae** (first secretary of Scottish FA):
- Criticized Sheffield style as merely "a system of long-kicking"
- Contrasted with Scots' technique of passing and close dribbling
- Effective lack of offside law separated Sheffield from modern soccer

### The Rouge System (Borrowed from Eton)

**Introduced**: 1859

**Definition** (1862 Sheffield rules):
- Two flags placed on goal line, one at either side of goal
- **12 feet from each post**
- Rouge scored "by the player who first touches the ball after it has been kicked between the rouge flags"

**Critical Detail**: Ball had to be **touched by hand** - similar to rugby try

**Comparison**: Australian Rules football only modern code still using concept (with "behind posts")

### Example: 1867 Youdan Cup Final

**Match**: Hallam won with two rouges to Norfolk's nil

**Bell's Life** reported Hallam's first rouge:
> "After half an hour's play the ball was kicked by Elliott, not through the goal, but just over it, and was touched down by Ash in splendid style, after running round two opponents before getting to the ball, thus securing a rouge."

**Significance**: Clearly demonstrates handling element - nothing in common with modern soccer

## Public School Influence on Sheffield Rules

### The Revisionist Denial

**Goulstone's Assertion**:
> "There is little about the Sheffield code to suggest a significant influence from the elite schools" because "none [of the first Sheffield clubs] had public school alumni as their driving force"

**Harvey's Claim**:
- Argued "against any decisive public school involvement" in development of Sheffield code

### Evidence of Public School Influence

#### 1. The Rouge System

- Direct borrowing from **Eton College's** "field" football game
- Demonstrates clear public school influence

#### 2. Social Context

**Harvey's Own Admission**: Sheffield FC was "a socially elite institution" with many privately educated members

**Middle-Class Emulation**:
> "Emulation of those they perceive to be their social superiors has long been a defining characteristic of the British middle classes and sport was no exception to this."

#### 3. Direct Research of Public School Rules

**Documented**: Leading members **Nathaniel Creswick** and **William Prest** wrote to leading public schools to obtain copies of their football rules

#### 4. Sporting Press Awareness

- Members would have read debates in *Bell's Life*, *The Field*, and other sporting newspapers
- Well aware of public school discussions about football

### Detailed Comparison: 1858 Sheffield Rules vs 1845 Rugby School Rules

**Harvey's Mistaken Claim**:
> "He could find no evidence that the Sheffield club's early rules were derived from examining the codes of various public schools"

**Reality**: Direct textual parallels exist

| Sheffield Rule (1858) | Rugby School Rule (1845) |
|----------------------|-------------------------|
| **Rule 1**: "Kick off from the middle must be a place kick" | **Rule vi**: "Kick off from middle must be a place" |
| **Rule 2**: "Kick out must not be from more than twenty five yards out of goal" | **Rule vii**: "Kick out must not be from more than ten yards out of goal if a place kick, not more than twenty five yards if a punt, drop, or knock on" |
| **Rule 3**: "Fair catch is a catch direct from the foot of the opposite side and entitles a free kick" | **Rule i**: "Fair catch is a catch direct from the foot" |
| **Rule 4**: "Charging is fair in case of a place kick, with the exception of kick off, as soon as a player offers to kick, but may always draw back unless he has actually touched the ball with his foot" | **Rule ix**: "Charging is fair in case of a place kick, as soon as the ball has touched the ground" |

#### Additional Links to Rugby School (Consolidated 1862 Rules)

**Sheffield Rule 8**: Forbidding ball being picked up from ground
- Commonly used by rugby clubs
- Appears in consolidated 1862 Rugby School rules

**Sheffield Rule 3**: Fair catch concept
- Ball caught on full = "fair catch"
- Term still used in American football
- Known as "mark" in rugby and Australian rules

**Sheffield Rule 9**: Bouncing ball may be stopped by hand
- Variation on Rugby School rule allowing bouncing ball to be caught

**Sheffield Rule 10**: "No goal may be kicked from touch, nor by free kick from a fair catch"
- Based on 1845 Rugby School rule 5
- Also allows goal from fair catch

**Sheffield Rule 11**: Defines when ball is in touch and how to return to play
- Same wording as Rugby's consolidated 1862 rules
- Exception: rugby player could also throw ball in to himself

**Sheffield Rule 6**: Prohibits "knocking on" with hand, penalized with free kick
- Appears with slightly different wording in Rugby rule 11 (1862)

#### Only Rules Without Rugby Link

**Sheffield Rules 5 and 7**: Forbidding pushing, hacking, tripping, holding, or pulling player over
- No link to Rugby School rules
- May suggest objection to roughness
- But many adult rugby clubs also banned hacking and tripping

### Collins' Conclusion on Public School Influence

**Refutation of Harvey/Swain Claim**:
> To make the claim that the first "Sheffield rules can be described as markedly anti-Rugby in form" is therefore simply mistaken.

**Important Caveat**:
- This does NOT suggest Sheffield football was version of Rugby School game
- Does NOT suggest origins were rugby-based
- Emphasizes **complexity** of "primordial soup" of early codes
- **Impossibility of drawing direct line** from early rules to modern codes

## Sheffield FC's Continued Rugby Playing

### Evidence of Mixed Code Playing

**1864**: Home and away matches against **Leeds rugby club**
- J.G. Hudson (Leeds founder): Rules "were of a mongrel type, neither rugby nor association"

**1868**: Played against Manchester
- Lost rugby match by one goal and eight touchdowns to nil
- Won home game by two rouges to nil

**1870**: Five Sheffield FC players appeared in **Yorkshire county rugby team**
- Played Lancashire at Leeds
- **First-ever rugby Roses Match**

### Significance

**Collins' Assessment**:
> "Contrary to the beliefs of the revisionists, Sheffield was not a bastion of soccer purity uninfluenced by the public schools. Its use of Eton's rouge, allowing the ball to be handled, its effectively non-existent offside law, and the similarity of a number of its rules to those of Rugby School demonstrate that its game was an intricate melange of old, new, and borrowed ideas about how to play an enjoyable game of football."

**Final Judgment**:
> "This is not to say that Sheffield football had no impact on the development of the game that came to be known as soccer – but there is no evidence to support the claim that Sheffield either saved or created modern association football."

## Other Regional Codes: The Case of Nottingham

### Nottingham Rules (1860s-1870s)

**Andrew Dawes' Research**: Nottingham rules may have been **closer to modern soccer** than Sheffield

**Key Features**:
- Appears to have forbidden **any handling** of ball by outfield players
- Became issue when Sheffield FC visited Nottingham
- Sheffield's limited handling/catching not acceptable

**Complexity**: Rugby-style **hacking** seems to have been acceptable in Nottingham game

**Significance**: "Reinforcing the futility of drawing direct links between early rules and modern football"

## The "Football Culture" Debate

### The Revisionist Claims

**Terminology Used**:
- John Goulstone: "widespread football culture" of "English town and village of late Georgian to early Victorian period"
- Roy Hay and Mel Smith: "a culture of football" before FA formation (1863)
- Peter Swain: "broad football culture" in Britain (1818-1840)

**Geographic Continuity Claim** (Goulstone):
> "One could quite convincingly trace soccer's main ancestral line before 1867 back to Sheffield in 1857 – and earlier still to football practised in South Yorkshire, particularly in the game's Pennine strongholds around Penistone and Holmfirth, in the 1840s"

### Collins' Critique: Definition Problem

**Central Issue**: None of these authors define what they mean by "football culture"

**Raymond Williams**:
> "Culture is one of the two or three most complicated words in the English language"

**Problem**: Revisionists use word across different historical periods without regard for how assumptions change

### Geographic Continuity Problems

**Penistone**:
- No team from Penistone played football in Sheffield during Sheffield FA period
- Claims of "stronghold" status unsupported

**Holmfirth**:
- Far from being soccer "stronghold"
- Became **bastion of rugby** and (from 1895) **rugby league**

**Rochdale**:
- Home to short-lived "Fear-Noughts" and "Body Guards" (1841)
- No professional soccer side until **1907**
- But **Rochdale Hornets** leading Lancashire rugby/rugby league club from 1870s

**Leicester Area** (Blaby, Enderby, Whetstone, Wigston):
- Identified by Goulstone as playing football in 1850s
- Area had **rugby tradition predating soccer**

### Quantification Problems

#### Harvey's Data (1830-1859)

**Claims**: 93 teams traced over 29 years

**Reality**: Only **58 matches** traced in 29-year period
- Average of **just 2 matches per year** across entire British Isles
- Hardly "widespread" or evidence of "broad football culture"
- Some data from advertisements for challenges in *Bell's Life*
- No indication how many challenge matches actually took place

**Teams with Longevity**: Only **4 sides** existed for more than single year

#### Goulstone's Data

**Claims**: "Modern-style eleven-a-side matches appear to have been fairly common by early years of Victoria's reign"

**Reality**: Only **4 examples** in 14 years (1838-1852)
- Only **18 matches** actually took place (1838-1859)
- Including 3 played by Edinburgh University teams

**Pattern**: Overwhelming majority of teams had no life beyond single match for which they'd been assembled

### Peter Swain's Response: "The Evidence Mounts"

**Graham Curry's Challenge**: Highlighted tiny number of clubs and matches

**Swain's Counter-Evidence**:
- Numerous prosecutions of youths/young men for playing informal football (1841-1851)
- Excellent supplement to Robert Malcolmson's *Popular Recreations in English Society 1700-1850* (1973)

**Swain's Assumption**:
> Because newspapers only reported matches involving law-breaking, "reasonable to assume there must have been other [matches]" where no offenses committed

**Collins' Response**:
- Historian cannot assume anything about events for which there is no evidence
- If football so popular in 1840s, why so few legal, organized matches reported?
- *Bell's Life* and local dailies carried reports of cricket, boxing, horse racing, many other sports
- But "almost nothing on 'football culture' across the country"

### Swain's Shift in Argument

**New Examples**: Football games at fetes, fairs, festivals, works outings, communal picnics (to late 1850s)

**Critical Difference**:
- These are **unorganized informal matches**
- NOT same as organized matches Harvey and Goulstone claimed were forerunners of modern soccer
- Swain does NOT suggest these are precursor of modern codes

**New Axis of Revisionist Case**:
- No longer claiming rules/organization were direct antecedents
- Now arguing sheer **number** of "football matches" proves "broad, tenacious and visible footballing culture throughout nineteenth-century Britain"

## Defining "Football Culture" Across Time

### Football Culture in the 1890s

**Components**:

1. **Supporters**:
   - Paid to watch matches regularly
   - Various degrees of involvement

2. **Media**:
   - Daily print coverage
   - Not just matches but affairs of football generally

3. **Administrators**:
   - Drew salaries
   - Organized and directed game off pitch
   - Club, regional, national levels

4. **Players**:
   - Elite paid contractual wages
   - Regular, professional basis

5. **Social Discourse**:
   - More than sum of parts
   - Taken place in daily social discourse of large sections of society
   - **Eric Hobsbawm**: Football became part of "lingua franca of working-class men" in late Victorian era

### Football in First Half of 19th Century

**Components Present**: None of the above

**No Supporters**:
- Almost no clubs
- No regular competitions
- No means to express local club or civic pride

**Minimal Media**:
- Short and highly irregular reports or advertisements only

**No Administrators**:
- No organization of the game
- No paid administrators

**No Professionalism**:
- Working-class players occasionally received payment (cash, kind, stake money)
- But professionalism did not exist as system

**Not Part of Everyday Life**:
- Did not occupy important part of everyday life and language

### Collins' Conclusion on "Culture"

**Assessment**:
> "Even taking the revisionists' evidence at face value, if there was a football culture before 1850 it shared nothing with that which had come into being by the 1900 except the name."

**Methodological Critique**:
- Without precise and objective definition, term "football culture" is **meaningless as tool of historical analysis**
- Use is "highly misleading" - implies seamless continuity between:
  - Wide variety of football games in 1840s
  - Commercial association football in 1890s

**Final Problem**:
> "Perhaps most tellingly, it also ignores the fact that even the very word 'football' in the nineteenth-century context is itself problematic for historians"

## Implications for Game Development

### Multiple Legitimate Perspectives

This historiographical debate demonstrates:

1. **Complexity of Origins**: No single, simple origin story for modern football

2. **Regional Variations**: Sheffield, Nottingham, London, public schools all contributed differently

3. **Rule Hybridity**: Early codes borrowed from multiple sources

4. **Social Class Dynamics**: Both elite (public school) and working-class influences real

5. **The "Primordial Soup"**: Early football was chaotic mixture resisting linear narratives

### What We Can Confidently Say About Sheffield

**Positive Contributions**:
- One of earliest clubs with sustained existence (1857+)
- Organized inter-club competitions (Youdan Cup, Cromwell Cup)
- Significant regional football scene in 1860s-1870s
- Eventually integrated into FA system (1877)

**Limitations**:
- Did not "save" or "create" the FA
- Rules were hybrid borrowing from multiple sources (Rugby School, Eton, local preferences)
- Effective lack of offside law separated it from modern game
- Rouge system with handling element moved away from modern soccer
- Continued playing rugby-style matches into late 1860s

**Accurate Assessment**:
- Important regional variation
- Part of broader, complex development
- Contributed to but did not determine modern association football
- Eventually adopted FA rules completely (1877)

### Methodological Lessons

**For Historians**:
1. Cannot simply trace "ancestral lines" by comparing written rules
2. Must define terms like "culture" precisely for each historical period
3. Cannot assume continuity without evidence
4. Must resist viewing past through present-day categories

**For Game Developers**:
1. Can draw on Sheffield's actual distinctive features (rouge system, lack of offside)
2. Can represent the hybrid, experimental nature of 1860s football
3. Can show Sheffield as important regional variant, not "origin" of modern game
4. Can incorporate the debate itself as part of historical authenticity
