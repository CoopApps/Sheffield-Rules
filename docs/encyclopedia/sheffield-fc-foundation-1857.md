## Sheffield's Industrial Revolution: Material Foundations for Football (1736-1857)

### Population Explosion

**1616**: 2,207 people

**1736**: ~10,000 (still just a market town)

**1801**: 30,000 (tripled in 65 years)

**Cause**: "Large numbers migrated from rural Nottinghamshire and Lincolnshire, **drawn by better wages in the growing steel industry**"

**Result**: Changed demography from "ancient working class families who had lived in Sheffield for centuries" to mixed population

---

### The Critical 1850s: Steel Boom Creates Football

**CURRY'S EXPLICIT CAUSATION**:

> "There appears to have been a **spurt in the steel trade around the 1850s**, which **may have been significant for football's appearance towards the end of the decade**"

**1850s Industrial Developments**:

**1851**: Victoria Station opens
- Transport infrastructure enabling inter-club matches
- Railway mania creating huge steel demand

**1856**: Brown's adopts Bessemer process
- First in Sheffield to use revolutionary steel production method
- Massive increase in production capacity
- Cheaper steel production

**1856**: Henry Bessemer announces process in London
- "Allowed iron to be converted to steel in greater quantities with no fuel required"
- Made procedure "much cheaper"
- Start of large-scale steel production

**1850s**: Major firms expand to larger premises
- **Firths**: Future owner Mark Firth (renowned philanthropist)
- **Vickers**: "**One of the family was a founder of Hallam FC in 1860**"
- **Brown's**: Took over enormous Atlas works

**Direct Connection**: Industrial expansion → prosperity → leisure time and resources → club formation

## "Saint Monday": The Labor Practice Behind Sheffield Football

### The "Little Mesters" System

**Who**: Independent cutlers who had earned right to set up on their own

**Working Pattern**: "Able to work at his own convenience **as long as he met his targets for the rest of the week**"

**The Practice**:

> "Hence he may have been able to observe the practice of **'Saint Monday'** - **taking that day off so as to compensate for having to work on Saturday** - but may also have been forced to work longer hours on Thursday and Friday if he was lagging behind in his work schedule"

**Significance**:
- Pre-existing working-class leisure tradition
- Monday free time built into Sheffield labor culture
- Football adapted to established patterns, didn't create them

**Connection to Professionalism**: Explains Monday matches for working-class players documented in James Lang entry

**Curry's Note**: "See Chapter Six" for more detail on Saint Monday

## Sheffield's Village Structure: Multiple Local Identities

### 1861 Report (The Builder, 21 September 1861)

**"The condition of our chief towns - Sheffield"**:

> "Thick, pulverous haze is spread over the city... [with] dead dogs and cats floating on the waters. Pailfuls of blood soak down on the surface of the ground the blood oozingly finds it way, together with faecal matter, into the river. Sheffield is as **devoid of the decencies of civilisation as it was in the Dark Ages**"

**Nickname**: "Smoky Sheffield"

### 1801 Aristocratic Perspective

**Lady Caroline Stuart-Wortley** (wife of 1st Baron Wharncliffe):

> "We were in Sheffield today and I never was in so stinking, dirty and savage a place. Altogether **I never witnessed a scene of more idleness and filth in my life**"

**Paradox**: Beautiful natural setting (seven hills, five rivers, Peak District proximity) but horrific industrial pollution

**Implication for Football**:
- Need for leisure and escape from brutal industrial conditions
- Recreation gaining importance for workers' health
- Football as relief valve

## Why Club Football DIDN'T Begin in London: The Public School Problem

### London Had Football First

**Curry**: "Indeed, the game was **clearly evident in the capital**, but was **largely the domain of former pupils of the more important public schools** there such as **Eton, Harrow, Westminster and Charterhouse**"

**Control**: "Ex-players dominated the football scene in the metropolis"

---

### The Fatal Flaw: Status Rivalry

**The Problem**:

> "However, the very fact that **former public schoolboys were involved almost certainly contributed to its initial downfall**. The **diversity of the rules in their establishments worked against any sort of compromise** in inter-club fixtures and, consequently, **severely restricted the development of a club football subculture in and around London**"

**Mechanism**: Each public school had different rules and wanted THEIR rules to become the standard

**"Status Rivalry"**: Competition between schools to have their particular form adopted by others

**Result**: Former Eton players wouldn't accept Rugby rules, Rugby players wouldn't accept Harrow rules, etc.

**Consequence**: **Could not play inter-club matches** = no club football culture could develop

---

### Public School Power Nationally

**Despite Alternative Football Existing**:

> "The **social prestige and, more particularly, power of former public schoolboys** meant that **it was they who decided football's future path** and it was they who **maintained control of the game** in its formative years"

**Academic Evidence**: Adrian Harvey and John Goulstone "have shown that **further forms of football, free of public school influence, existed**"

**But**: Public school power through "social prestige" meant they controlled national development despite not being majority of players

---

### The Seven Boarding Schools That Controlled Football

**Schools That Mattered**:
1. Eton
2. Winchester
3. Harrow
4. Charterhouse
5. Westminster
6. Shrewsbury
7. Rugby

**Why Boarding Mattered**:

St. Paul's and Merchant Taylor's were excluded because they were day schools:

> "Those two schools were **not boarding establishments** and could not, therefore, provide pupils with the opportunity for recreation in any **additional spare time created by weekends and after school**. In short, the **day pupils had little chance to bring their opinions to bear in a 'melting pot' of contrasting football rules**"

**Implication**: Spare time essential for rule development and debate

**Sheffield Parallel**: Working-class Sheffield had spare time through **Saint Monday** - different mechanism, same enabling condition

## Folk Football's Incompatibility Problem

### Pre-Modern Football Everywhere

**Nature**: "Eclectic mix creating a sport form which was **somewhat different in each geographical location**"

**The Problem**: "**Ponderous rate of diffusion** because of the **problematic of engaging against teams from other areas**"

**Cause**: "Each town or village determined to **retain its own traditional practices**"

**Result**: Traditional folk football **could not scale** - no standardization = no inter-area matches

---

### Codification as Solution

**1845**: Rugby School issues **first written rules**
- "Changing the nature and form of regulation of a game which had **previously only been controlled by verbal agreement**"

**1847**: Eton Field Game produces transcribed laws
- "Many of which were **directly opposed to those of Rugby**"

**1849**: Surrey Football Club (London) creates rules

**Cambridge University**: Multiple rule sets for intra-university competition

**Advantage**: "Codification or writing down of the rules, allowing them to be **more easily absorbed and implemented** by other groups of participants"

**1850s**: "These embryonic forms of Association and Rugby football... were therefore subsequently spread, **predominantly by ex-public schoolboys**, into the wider society **during the 1850s**"

---

### Nature of the Diaries

**Scope**: "Creswick's diaries are substantial"

**Limitation**: "Only tend to **provide confirmation rather than new evidence** in the Sheffield football debate"

**Value**: Confirming existing theories with contemporary first-hand accounts

---

### Diary Entry 1: Foundation Confirmed (31 December 1857)

**Entry**:

> *I have established a foot ball club to which most of young Sheffield come and kick.*

#### Analysis: Foundation Date

**Curry's Interpretation**: "These diaries appear to confirm that Sheffield Football Club had been **established just before this date** and this fits in with the club's **official foundation date of 24 October 1857**"

**Alternative Theory Negated**: "Also appears to **negate any thought that the club had been established as early as 1855**"

**Significance**: Documentary evidence confirming 24 October 1857 as foundation date

---

#### Analysis: "Foot Ball" (Two Words)

**Phrase**: "**'Foot ball'** - rather than a single word"

**Significance**: "Emphasises the fact that the **foot was used in preference to the hand**, or indeed any other part of the body"

**Context in Wider Football Development**: "Important in the wider picture of the development of the game as it began to **polarise in terms of the amount of use of feet and hands**"

**Culmination**: "This would culminate in **December 1863** when the handlers, those following mainly Rugby School practices of play, **split with the embryonic Association followers** who preferred minimal handling and favoured use of the feet"

**Sheffield's Position**: "Confirms Sheffield's preferred code as one which **emphasised use of the feet**"

**Implication**: From the very beginning (1857), Sheffield football was kicking-based, not handling-based - six years before FA/Rugby split

---

#### Analysis: Popularity

**"Most of young Sheffield come and kick"**

**Significance**:
- Not small exclusive group
- Broad participation from "young Sheffield"
- Open membership, not restrictive
- Immediate popularity

**Contrast with London**: Public school clubs restricted to former pupils of specific schools
