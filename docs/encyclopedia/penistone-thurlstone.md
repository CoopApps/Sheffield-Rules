# Penistone and Thurlstone: The Rural Football Heartland

## Overview

**Location**: Approximately 15 miles north of Sheffield, 8 miles west of Barnsley

Two interconnected villages in South Yorkshire that maintained a thriving mob/folk football culture and played a crucial role in the development of Sheffield Rules football. The area produced three key figures who brought rural football traditions to Sheffield: **John Charles Shaw**, **John Marsh**, and **John Ness Dransfield**.

---

## Geographic and Economic Context

### Penistone

**Current Population** (2016): Approximately 7,000

**Historical Population**:
- 1831: 703
- Smaller than Thurlstone in early 19th century

**Geography**:
- Market town situated on a hill
- Struggled for consistent water supply due to elevation
- Agricultural economy as main source of income

**Transformation**:
- **1845**: Sheffield/Manchester railway station constructed
- Became busy junction with frequent trains to/from:
  - Sheffield
  - Manchester
  - Huddersfield
- Railway transformed fortunes, allowing Penistone to overtake Thurlstone in prosperity and population by 1880s

**Key Institutions** (*Post Office Directory of Yorkshire*, 1851):
- Church - "centre of spiritual, pastoral and academic provision within the district"
- Free Grammar School - educational center for the area

### Thurlstone

**Current Population** (2016): Approximately 1,400

**Historical Population**:
- 1831: 1,599
- Larger than Penistone in early 19th century

**Geography**:
- Village just west of Penistone
- In the valley of the River Don
- Capitalized on location with water access

**Economy**:
- Established mills and water-driven industry
- Economically richer than Penistone in early 19th century
- *Post Office Directory* (1851): "Thurlstone is the largest and most populous township in the parish and Union of Penistone"

**Decline**:
- After railway arrived at Penistone (1845), Thurlstone's advantage diminished
- Lost population and prosperity lead to Penistone by 1880s

## Football Culture

### Thriving Mob/Folk Football Tradition

**1844**:
- Three separate challenges issued by footballers of Thurlstone for matches against local rivals

**1845**:
- Reference notes group of Thurlstone men issuing challenge to play opponents from Southouse or Hepworth
- **Critical specification**: They were insistent they would only play "a game of foot-ball and not hand-ball"
- Shows clear distinction already existed between kicking and handling codes

### Characteristics of Penistone/Thurlstone Football

**Ball Size**:
- **Small ball** used (per Dransfield)
- "Blown up with bladders"
- Contrasted with large balls of Derby/Ashbourne mob football

**Style of Play**:
- **Predominantly kicking variety** (dribbling and minimal handling)
- Rural game, not street game
- Played on open spaces like The Fairfields

**Contrast with Derby/Ashbourne**:
- Derby/Ashbourne used **large ball** = "hugball" (predominantly handling)
- Penistone/Thurlstone used **small ball** = kicking game
- Shows football already splitting into "kickers" vs. "handlers" camps by 1840s

### Historical Evidence of Two Football Types (1827)

**Derby, Shrove Tuesday 1827** (per Graham Curry):

1. **Mob/folk football** between parishes of St. Peter's and All Saints
   - Large ball, handling game

2. **Earlier that day**: Mickleover vs. Littleover (villages, now Derby suburbs)
   - Contest at "kick-ball" rather than handling form
   - Shows two distinct forms existed simultaneously

**Significance**: Although both fell under blanket term "football", game had already begun split into two camps.

## The Penistone/Thurlstone Axis of Influence

### Three Prominent Individuals

The spread of Penistone/Thurlstone football to Sheffield lay in hands of three individuals:

1. **John Charles Shaw**
   - Co-founder of Hallam FC
   - From Penistone district
   - Sheffield FA President (1869-1883)
   - Captain of Hallam when they won Youdan Cup (1867)

2. **John Marsh**
   - First captain of Wednesday club
   - From Penistone district
   - Captain when Wednesday won Cromwell Cup (1868)

3. **John Ness Dransfield**
   - "The unsung catalyst"
   - Born 26 October 1839 in Penistone
   - Most influential but least recognized
   - Brought educational and football experience from multiple institutions

**Geographic Significance** (per Curry):
> "Penistone area played significant part in development of modern club football in Sheffield"

### Why This Mattered

**Figurational Sociology Perspective**:
- Not mono-causal explanation
- Multiple influences on long-term social processes
- Three processes/avenues of diffusion at work in Sheffield:
  1. Sheffield Collegiate School former pupils
  2. Public school influence (minimal but present)
  3. **Penistone/Thurlstone folk football traditions**

**Impact on Sheffield**:
- Presence of these three individuals partly explains why modern form took hold swiftly in Sheffield
- Brought rural kicking game traditions to urban setting
- Influenced type of game played in and around city
- Helped create pre-soccer-like form in Sheffield

## Educational Institutions

### Penistone Free Grammar School (1837-1855)

**Location**: Built on land called Kirk Flatt or The Fairfields

**Headmaster**: Reverend Samuel Sunderland (1837-1855)
- Vicar of Penistone
- Born in Wakefield
- Attended Clare College, Cambridge (close to Trinity College)
- Trinity College regarded as centre of sporting, particularly football, debate and diffusion
- Baptised Shaw, Marsh, and Dransfield

**The Fairfields**:
- Part of original endowment land
- Portion eventually given to Manchester, Sheffield and Lincolnshire Railway
- Railway completed route between Sheffield and Dunford Bridge (just west of Penistone/Thurlstone) in 1845
- **Location of football play**: Very important piece of land for school and boys
- Cockpit for cockfighting located there
- Likely where Dransfield had first taste of Penistone football
- "Dribbling and minimal handling form of the game"
- Dransfield notes Shaw playing football on Fairfields opposite his father's offices when Shaw was clerk there

**1851 Commissioners**:
- When commissioners for 1851 exhibition wrote wanting worthy representatives
- Sunderland's name most prominent
- Much loved figure who brought success to school

**Sunderland's Death** (1855):
- Thrown from carriage at Rowsley, Derbyshire during Sunday school outing to Peak District
- Dransfield was walking off cricket ground in Penistone when he heard news
- Bitter blow for Dransfield and entire community

**Students**:
- John Ness Dransfield (1847-1852)
- John Charles Shaw (as clerk at Dransfield's father's offices)
- John Marsh (baptised by Sunderland)

## Key Locations for Football

### The Fairfields (Kirk Flatt)

**Ownership**: Land bequeathed in original endowment to Penistone Grammar School

**Activities**:
- Football playing ground for grammar school boys
- Cockfighting cockpit located there
- Open space for sport and recreation

**Dransfield's Memory**:
- Presented seven cockspurs (belonging to his uncle) to school museum in 1913
- Could have been participator in cockfighting
- First taste of Penistone football on Fairfields
- Witnessed Shaw playing football there when Shaw was clerk at his father's offices

**Historical Significance**:
- Training ground for future Sheffield football leaders
- Where Penistone kicking game was learned and practiced
- Direct connection to Shaw's football education

## The Cambridge Connection

### Trinity College and Clare College

**Trinity College**:
- Regarded as centre of sporting, particularly football, debate and diffusion
- Rev. Sunderland attended nearby Clare College
- Proximity meant exchange of sporting ideas

**Football Diffusion Center**:
- Cambridge University central to football ideas, rules, and diffusion (per Graham Curry)
- Multiple Penistone-connected teachers attended Cambridge:
  - Rev. Samuel Sunderland - Clare College
  - George Rider (Grenoside) - St. Catharine's College
  - George Hale Puckle - St. John's College (1843-1851)
  - Benjamin Atkinson Irving - St. John's (1846-1847) and Emmanuel College (1847-1854)

**Edgar Montagu Connection**:
- Old Salopian (former Shrewsbury School pupil)
- Contemporary of Rev. Sunderland at both Shrewsbury and Cambridge (Magdalene College)
- Part of group that framed first Cambridge football rules around 1840

## Academic Analysis

### Three Strands of Diffusion to Sheffield Football

**1. Sheffield Collegiate School**:
- Former pupils including Nathaniel Creswick (co-founder Sheffield FC)
- 17 of original 57 Sheffield FC members attended Collegiate
- School played kicking/Association-style game

**2. Public School Influence**:
- Minimal but present
- Sheffield footballers wrote to major public schools for rules
- "Rouge" scoring system likely from Eton College
- Hard to understand rules created barrier to diffusion

**3. Penistone/Thurlstone Axis** (Central tenet):
- Folk football traditions from rural South Yorkshire
- Three key individuals: Shaw, Marsh, Dransfield
- Brought small ball, kicking game to Sheffield
- Most significant but least studied influence

### Figurational Sociology Perspective

**No Mono-Causal Explanation**:
- Multiple influences on long-term social processes
- Not "cultural dopes" - Sheffield footballers aware of all sources
- Conscious realization of public school existence even if not adopted wholesale

**Why Sheffield?**:
- Complex combination of:
  - Collegiate School educated middle class
  - Awareness of public school practices
  - **Rural folk football traditions via Penistone/Thurlstone individuals**

## Legacy and Historical Significance

### Rapid Growth of Sheffield Football Subculture

**Late 1850s - Early 1860s**:
- Sheffield Football Club formed 1857 (first club formed solely for football)
- Rapid spread of organized football in city
- Pre-soccer-like form took hold swiftly

**Why This Location?**:
- Multiple avenues of diffusion converged
- Penistone/Thurlstone individuals brought tested folk football traditions
- Traditions merged with Collegiate School education and public school awareness
- Created unique Sheffield style

### The Small Ball / Large Ball Distinction

**Two Types of Football by 1840s**:

1. **Rural, Small Ball, Kicking Game**:
   - Penistone, Thurlstone
   - Mickleover vs. Littleover (Derby area, 1827)
   - Became basis for Association football/soccer

2. **Street/Mob, Large Ball, Handling Game**:
   - Derby, Ashbourne
   - "Hugball" - predominantly handling
   - Became basis for rugby football

**Historical Importance**:
- Shows split occurred before public school codification
- Folk football already differentiating by geography and culture
- Penistone/Thurlstone on "kicking" side of divide

### Geographic Importance

**Curry's Assessment**:
> "Penistone area played significant part in development of modern club football in Sheffield"

**Evidence**:
- Two of three major trophy winners from Penistone area:
  - John Charles Shaw (Hallam, Youdan Cup 1867)
  - John Marsh (Wednesday, Cromwell Cup 1868)
- John Dransfield's "unsung" influence
- Folk football traditions provided foundation

## Academic Significance: Why Modern Club Football Began in Sheffield

### Three Strands of Diffusion (Summary)

The development of early Sheffield football resulted from **three interconnected strands of diffusion**:

1. **Sheffield Collegiate School Former Pupils**
   - Including Nathaniel Creswick (co-founder Sheffield FC)
   - 17 of original 57 Sheffield FC members attended Collegiate
   - Brought public school-influenced kicking game

2. **Public School Influence** (Minimal but Present)
   - Sheffield footballers wrote to major public schools for rules
   - "Rouge" scoring system likely adopted from Eton College
   - Rules were hard to understand, creating barrier to diffusion
   - Influence present but largely indirect

3. **Penistone/Thurlstone Folk Football** (Most Significant but Least Studied)
   - **Shaw, Marsh, and Dransfield** as vectors of diffusion
   - Brought thriving rural folk football culture to Sheffield
   - Small ball, kicking game traditions
   - Informal structure built around mob/folk challenges

### Curry and Dunning's Framework

Three further elements confirmed as important to diffusion:

1. **Influence of Former Public Schoolboys**
   - Through teachers like Turner (Rugby), Puckle and Irving (Cambridge)
   - Indirect influence through educational institutions

2. **Importance of Local Sporting Elites**
   - Sheffield FC as elite organization
   - Gentlemen's club with social gatekeeping
   - Marsh's trade (engraver) not socially acceptable to Sheffield FC elite

3. **Significance of Educational Institutions**
   - Grammar schools (Penistone Free Grammar School)
   - Boarding schools (Grenoside, Liverpool, Windermere)
   - Universities (Cambridge colleges as diffusion centers)

### The Penistone/Thurlstone Contribution

**Question**: Were Shaw, Marsh, and Dransfield more significant than Nathaniel Creswick?

**Answer**:
- Problematic to claim they were **more** significant than Creswick
- However, **one of the reasons** Sheffield footballers found themselves at forefront of game's rapid rise (1860s-1870s) was:
  - Existence of thriving mob/folk football scene in nearby Penistone and Thurlstone
  - Villages were part of football figuration
  - As ties of multiple interdependency lengthened in mid-19th century England
  - Allowed players from outlying rural settlements to influence events in rapidly growing cities

**1830s-1840s Penistone/Thurlstone**:
- Little or no formal organization
- But clearly existed informal structure built around:
  - Past mob/folk challenges (1844-1845)
  - Positive Muscular Christian (Rev. Sunderland) who actively promoted physical exercise
  - The Fairfields as playing space
  - Small ball, kicking game tradition

### Dransfield's Educational Experience: Most Significant Finding

**Greatest Significance**: New evidence on Dransfield's life, especially educational experience

**Most Important Data**: Backgrounds of Dransfield's footballing mentors:
- **Rev. Samuel Sunderland** (Penistone, Cambridge)
- **Rev. Dawson William Turner** (Liverpool, Rugby School, Oxford)
- **George Hale Puckle** (Liverpool/Windermere, Cambridge, Thring contemporary)
- **Benjamin Atkinson Irving** (Liverpool/Windermere, Sheffield Collegiate, Cambridge)

**All with public school and/or university footballing connections**

**Critical Point**: These mentors were **complementing his previous experiences gained in his native village**

**Result**: Proved vital for diffusion of game in South Yorkshire

### Unintended Consequences

**Myriad of social processes** led to unintended consequence:
- Galvanized further an already vibrant footballing subculture
- Subculture created in Sheffield largely by former pupils of Collegiate School
- Penistone/Thurlstone influence amplified existing Sheffield football culture
- Created unique combination of:
  - Elite Collegiate School educated middle class
  - Public school rules awareness
  - Rural folk football traditions
  - Cambridge compromise rules via educated masters

### Answer to the Question

**"Why did modern club football begin in Sheffield?"**

**A large part of the answer should include reference to events in and around the villages of Penistone and Thurlstone.**

**Components of Answer**:

1. **Thriving folk football culture** in Penistone/Thurlstone
   - Small ball, kicking game
   - 1844-1845 challenges showing "foot-ball not hand-ball" distinction
   - Informal structure around mob/folk tradition

2. **Three key individuals** bringing this culture to Sheffield:
   - John Charles Shaw (Hallam FC co-founder, Sheffield FA President 15+ years)
   - John Marsh (Wednesday FC co-founder, Sheffield Association captain)
   - John Ness Dransfield (unsung catalyst with comprehensive football education)

3. **Educational network** via Dransfield:
   - Cambridge-educated masters (Sunderland, Puckle, Irving)
   - Exposure to multiple football codes (folk, Rugby handling, Cambridge kicking)
   - Bridge between rural tradition and urban organization

4. **Sheffield Collegiate School** former pupils:
   - Nathaniel Creswick and others
   - Created organized club structure (Sheffield FC, 1857)

5. **Geographic proximity and railway access**:
   - Penistone 15 miles north of Sheffield
   - 1845 railway connection facilitated movement
   - Players could participate in Sheffield while maintaining Penistone connections

6. **Social and economic factors**:
   - Growing industrial city with leisure time
   - Middle-class participants with education and organization skills
   - Working-class players bringing folk traditions (though limited by social gatekeeping)

### Not the Only Area

**Important Caveat**: Sheffield was not only area of footballing importance

**Other Contemporary Developments**:

**London** (Same period):
- Clubs formed solely for football
- Forest club in Snaresbrook/Leytonstone area
- Metropolitan development parallel to Sheffield

**Melbourne, Australia** (Late 1850s):
- Beginning of organized structure
- What became Australian Rules Football
- Commenced during same decade

**Global Context**: Modern football developing simultaneously in multiple locations with different local influences

### Figurational Sociology Perspective

**No Mono-Causal Explanation**:
- Not "cultural dopes" unaware of other influences
- Conscious of multiple football traditions
- Actively synthesized different elements

**Long-Term Social Processes**:
- Multiple interdependencies lengthening
- Rural to urban migration and influence
- Educational institutions as diffusion vectors
- Class dynamics and social gatekeeping
- Geographic ties and transport networks

**Complex Figuration**:
- Sheffield FC elite + Collegiate School education
- Penistone/Thurlstone folk tradition + rural kicking game
- Cambridge compromise rules + public school awareness
- = Unique Sheffield football culture

### Legacy

**Penistone and Thurlstone's contribution to modern football**:

1. Provided three key individuals who shaped Sheffield football
2. Brought rural kicking game traditions to urban setting
3. Informal folk football structure complemented formal club organization
4. Geographic connection maintained through Dransfield's educational journey
5. Demonstrated how rural traditions could influence urban development
6. Helped create conditions for Sheffield's football prominence in 1860s-1870s

**Answer**: When asked "why did modern club football begin in Sheffield?", the answer must include the Penistone/Thurlstone contribution alongside Sheffield Collegiate School and Cambridge/public school influences.
