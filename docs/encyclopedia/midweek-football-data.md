# Midweek Football in Sheffield (1876-1886): Research Data

## Overview

Detailed study of midweek football matches in Sheffield and surrounding districts for the seasons 1876-77 to 1885-86, providing empirical evidence of Saint Monday tradition and working-class leisure patterns in Victorian England.

**Research by**: Graham Curry

**Published in**: "Football Spectatorship in mid-to-late Victorian Sheffield", *Soccer & Society*, 2007

---

## Research Methodology

### Data Sources

**Primary Source**: *Sheffield and Rotherham Independent* (extensively trawled)

**Secondary Source**: *Sheffield Daily Telegraph* (occasional additional source)

**Caveat**: More games may have been played which went unreported

### Inclusion Criteria

**Matches Included**:
- Only matches specifically mentioning day of match in newspaper report
- Season period: **September 1 to March 31** (recognized Victorian season length)
- Connected to cricket popularity in city and Yorkshire as whole

**Seasonal Pattern**:
- Little or no football activity before beginning of October
- Mere **6 midweek games reported in April** across all 10 surveyed seasons
- Shows football as winter sport, cricket as summer sport

### Exclusions

**Matches Excluded** (to avoid inflation of midweek figures):

1. **Boxing Day** (26 December)
   - Recognized holiday date
   - Attendances would have increased
   - Players and spectators would have agreed free time

2. **New Year's Day** (1 January)
   - Recognized holiday date
   - Same reasoning as Boxing Day

3. **Shrove Tuesday** (dates varied)
   - Traditional festival time
   - Recognized holiday
   - But activity substantial and worthy of further study

**Shrove Tuesday Example**:
- **5 March 1878** (Shrove Tuesday): **13 matches** recorded in Sheffield and Rotherham Independent
- May show continuation of mob/folk football custom at traditional festival times
- Deserving of further study beyond this article's scope

#### Analysis of Table 1

**Monday Dominance**:
- 294 of 413 matches (71.2%)
- **Clear Saint Monday pattern**
- Overwhelming majority of midweek football on first day of work week
- Shows working-class taking Monday for leisure

**Wednesday Pattern** (42 matches, 10.2%):
- Many school and college fixtures
- Sheffield Collegiate School, Spinkhill College, St Matthias, Broom Park
- Montgomery College, Wesley College, Sheffield Royal Grammar
- Sheffield Medical Students
- Practice of Wednesday afternoon sport continues to 21st century

**Thursday Pattern** (41 matches, 9.9%):
- Thursday Wanderers particularly active (18 total midweek matches)
- 17 games on Thursday in 1876-77, 1877-78, 1878-79 seasons alone
- Middle-class professionals playing Thursday afternoon

**Tuesday** (33 matches, 8.0%):
- Less common than Monday or Wednesday/Thursday
- But still significant

**Friday Rarely Used** (3 matches, 0.7%):
- Only 3 matches across 10 years
- End of work week not popular for football
- Workers likely exhausted or preparing for weekend

**Seasonal Variation**:
- **High**: 1876-77 (72 matches), 1884-85 (57 matches)
- **Low**: 1882-83 (26 matches), 1880-81 (30 matches)
- Shows fluctuation in midweek football activity
- Possible economic factors, weather, or changing work patterns

## Detailed Club Analyses

### Sheffield Wednesday FC

**Founded**: 1867 (from existing cricket club)

**Status by 1876-1886**: Pre-eminent football organization in city

**Midweek Record**: 34 matches (2nd most active)

**High-Attendance Matches**: 8 of 18 (most of any club)

**Characteristics**:
- Thriving cup team locally and nationally
- Growing from already existing cricket club
- Most successful side in Sheffield around this time
- Drew largest crowds for competitive matches
- FA Cup runs particularly popular

**Attendances**:
- vs Blackburn Rovers (Huddersfield): 12,500
- vs Blackburn Rovers (Manchester): 10,000
- vs Upton Park: 4,500
- vs Lockwood Brothers: 3,500
- vs Preston North End: 3,000
- vs Notts County: 3,000
- vs Heeley: 1,200
- vs Queen's Park: 1,000

**1886-87 Professionalism Crisis**:
- Administrators dithered about sanctioning professionalism
- Submitted FA Cup entry form too late
- Several players threw in lot with Lockwood Brothers
- Lockwood Brothers reached FA Cup 5th round
- **Pressured Wednesday committee** into recognizing need for professionalism
- Had to turn professional to attract better players

**Significance**: Shows transition from amateur to professional era

### Thursday Wanderers FC

**Active Period**: Particularly 1876-77, 1877-78, 1878-79

**Pattern**: **17 games on Thursday afternoon** in these three seasons alone

**Midweek Record**: 18 total matches (10th most active)

**Revival Attempt** (1882-83):
- Noted in Sheffield and Rotherham Independent
- Only amounted to one fixture vs invitation eleven
- Attendance: "quite small"
- Revival failed

**Social Class Composition**:
- **Vast majority middle-class professionals** in and around Sheffield
- 1876: Strong contingent from Sheffield FC
- Sheffield FC known for antipathy towards professionalism
- Championing of amateur ethos

**Employment Profile**:
- Well-connected people in city
- Well-paid, secure employment
- Often connected to steel industry

**Example - Sorby Family** (1876):
- Three players from Sorby family in one fixture
- All successful businessmen in Sheffield
- Shows family networks in middle-class football

**Importations**:

*December 1882 Match at Bramall Lane*:
- Imported **Henry (H.A.) Cursham** from Nottingham
  - Already capped by England
  - Would represent Corinthians (famous amateur club)
  - Ex-public school and university men
  - Propounded amateur values: fair play, participation over winning
- Imported **Arthur (A.W.) Cursham** from Nottingham
  - Already capped by England
  - Brother of Henry

*February 1879*:
- Club **criticized in local press** for persistent importations
- Shows competitive edge even among amateurs
- Willing to import quality players for prestige

**Sheffield FC Connection**:
- Strong connection remained
- Newspaper reporter referred to Thursday Wanderers as **"Sheffield"** when noting they won toss
- Essentially same club/personnel?

**High-Attendance Match**:
- Only 1 match over 1000: Thursday 21 Oct 1880 vs Queen's Park (1,000)
- Despite 18 midweek matches, only this one drew large crowd
- Shows Thursday not popular day for spectators

**Significance**:
- **Different class using different day than working-class Monday**
- Professional/business autonomy allowed midweek leisure
- But still midweek football, still taking time from work
- Shows Saint Monday not exclusive to working class
- Middle class also challenged work week boundaries

## The Sheffield Zulus Phenomenon (1879-1882)

### Origins and Format

**Years Active**: Between 1879 and 1882

**Concept**: Group of Sheffield footballers played matches dressed as members of Southern African Zulu tribe

**Topical Context**: Anglo-Zulu War (1879)

**Initial Purpose**:
- **First game**: For benefit of dependents of men killed during Anglo-Zulu War
- Charitable purpose
- Topical military theme

**Evolution**:
- As novelty became more popular
- Players began charging entrance fees
- **Seemingly split profits among themselves**
- **Rewarded themselves** from proceeds

### The Ban and Reinstatement

**Local Association Response**:
- Practice cut short by local association
- **Number of players banned**

**Quick Reinstatement**:
- **Quickly reinstated** following appropriate apologies
- Shows authorities willing to forgive
- Perhaps recognized charitable origins

### Significance for Professionalism

**May Constitute Early Form of Soccer Professionalism**:
- Players receiving money directly from football
- Payment from gate receipts
- Sharing profits among players
- Pre-dates FA's legalization of professionalism (1885)
- Sheffield evidence of professionalism before FA approval

**Important Caveat**:
- Extensive trawls of Sheffield and Rotherham Independent
- **No mention unearthed** of payment to players in other midweek games
- Zulus appear unique in this regard (besides later Wednesday/Heeley gate receipt speculation)

### High-Profile Match

**Date**: Monday 10 November 1879

**Match**: Sheffield XI vs Sheffield Zulus

**Venue**: Bramall Lane

**Attendance**: Between **1,500 and 2,000**
- Specially arranged encounter
- One of high-attendance midweek matches
- Shows public interest in novelty

**Significance**: Novelty matches could draw crowds comparable to competitive fixtures

## The Pantomimists Charity Matches

### Format and Participants

**Participants**:
- **Actors** performing at two local theatres in Sheffield
- **vs Garrick club** (named after David Garrick)
- David Garrick: Famous Shakespearean actor of late 18th century
- Fitting theatrical connection

**Venue**: Bramall Lane

**Purpose**: Proceeds donated to **three local charities**

**Appropriateness**: Actors playing Garrick club - theatrical naming

### The Matches

**First Match - Monday 15 February 1886**:
- Attendance: **20,000 spectators**
- **Largest recorded midweek attendance in entire study**
- **Largest crowd for any match in this period**
- Phenomenal turnout for charity match

**Second Match - Monday 23 February 1885**:
- Attendance: **5,500 spectators**
- Still very large for midweek match
- Second charity match a year earlier

### Social Significance

**Reporter's Critical Observation** (1886 match):
> "A host of 'horny-handed sons of toil' (many of whom were perhaps devotees of St. Monday) had gathered"

**Importance of This Quote**:
- **Direct reference to Saint Monday in Sheffield press**
- Shows local populace aware of practice
- May even signal **degree of acceptance**
- Not condemnation but observation
- Perhaps even approval in context of charity

**"Horny-Handed Sons of Toil" Explained**:
- Working-class men
- Manual laborers
- Hands hardened by physical work
- Phrase used sympathetically, not condescendingly

**Class Composition**:
- **Predominantly working-class attendance**
- But charity nature may have attracted broader audience
- 20,000 attendance shows massive cross-class appeal

### The Reporter

**Possibly Same Reporter** as 1890 Sheffield Derby:
- Similar phrase used: "horny-handed sons of toil"
- 1886 Pantomimists match
- 1890 First Sheffield Derby (Wednesday vs United)
- Same documentary style
- Same awareness of class composition
- Same sympathetic tone toward working class

**Shows**:
- Consistency in Sheffield press coverage
- Awareness of Saint Monday practice
- Documentation of working-class leisure

### Significance

**Largest Crowds**:
- 20,000 unprecedented for midweek match
- Shows football's mass appeal
- Charity purpose legitimized midweek attendance
- Perhaps easier to justify to employers

**Cross-Class Appeal**:
- Actors (middle-class professionals)
- vs Footballers (Garrick club)
- For charity (respectable cause)
- Drew working-class "horny-handed sons of toil"
- Shows football transcending class boundaries

**Saint Monday Legitimized**:
- Charity purpose made Saint Monday acceptable
- 20,000 people absent from work on Monday
- For good cause
- Shows contested nature of work/leisure boundary

## Attendance Patterns and Spectator Language

### Vague Newspaper Language

**Most Match Reports**: No mention of attendance figures

**Common Imprecise Phrases**:

1. **"A numerous and respectable company"**
   - Example: Sheffield FC v Notts., Thursday 20 December 1877
   - "Respectable" suggests middle-class attendance
   - Or behavior considered appropriate

2. **"A large concourse of spectators"**
   - Example: Staveley v Heeley, Monday 20 January 1880
   - No numerical indication
   - Just "large"

3. **"A fair muster"**
   - Example: Heeley v Derby Midland, Monday 30 November 1885
   - Suggests moderate attendance
   - Not large, not small

**Limitations**:
- Impossible to quantify exactly
- Shows reporting conventions of era
- Numerical precision not priority

### Accurate Reporters

**Some Correspondents More Precise**:

1. **"Nearly 1,000 spectators"**
   - Heeley vs Hallam, Monday 2 December 1878
   - Local derby
   - Specific number given

2. **"Between 1,500 or 2,000"**
   - Sheffield XI vs Sheffield Zulus, Monday 10 November 1879
   - Range given
   - Shows estimation method

3. **"1,500 spectators"**
   - Sheffield Association trial, Monday 30 October 1876
   - Representative match
   - Specific number

4. **"4,500"**
   - Wednesday vs Upton Park, Tuesday 7 February 1882
   - FA Cup tie at Bramall Lane
   - Precise figure

### Special Trains to Matches

**FA Cup Semi-Final** (Wednesday v Blackburn Rovers):
- Match at Huddersfield: 12,500 attendance
- Replay at Whalley Range Ground, Manchester: 10,000 attendance
- **Several special trains ran from Sheffield** for both matches
- Shows commitment of supporters
- Willing to travel midweek
- Willing to pay train fare + admission
- Taking time off work for important matches

**Significance**:
- Railway companies recognized demand
- Organized special services
- Football driving transportation industry
- Midweek travel for leisure

### What Attendances Demonstrate

**Substantial Numbers**:
- "Good deal of football taking place in midweek"
- "Substantial numbers of players involved"
- **"More particularly, substantial numbers of spectators"**

**Pattern Clear**:
- High-profile clubs (Wednesday, Heeley) drew large crowds
- **Monday dominant** for high attendances (13 of 18)
- Even trial matches could draw 1,000-3,000
- FA Cup ties drew largest crowds (4,500-12,500)
- Charity matches broke all records (20,000)

**Implication**:
- **People absent from work to attend**
- Either Saint Monday tradition allowed this
- Or they took time off specifically for football
- Either way: **Leisure taking precedence over work** for some
- Employers either tolerating or unable to prevent
- Football as contested social space

## The 1890 First Sheffield Derby - Saint Monday Persists

### Context and Timing

**Date**: Monday 15 December 1890

**Teams**: Sheffield Wednesday vs Sheffield United

**Historical Significance**: **First Sheffield derby** between the two clubs

**"Bull Week" Context**:
- Days before Christmas
- Workers **registered as many hours as possible**
- Goal: **Increase wages** for bumper pay packet
- Carry them through Christmas and New Year break
- Intensive working period

**Strange Decision**:
- **Why hold match on Monday during bull week?**
- Most people should be busy augmenting wages
- Choosing work over leisure for financial gain
- Yet match still scheduled for Monday

### Attendance and Result

**Crowd**: **More than 10,000 supporters**

**Despite Bull Week**: 10,000+ still turned out
- Forgoing potential wages to attend
- Football more important than extra money
- **Maintains Saint Monday tradition** in Sheffield
- Even when economic incentive to work

**Significance**:
- Shows deep cultural embedding of practice
- Not just opportunistic leisure
- Football as cultural priority
- Willingness to sacrifice income for sport

### Social Composition of Crowd

**Reporter's Observation**:
> "Though the horny-handed sons of toil predominated in the throng, there were representatives of other classes"

**Working-Class Predominance**:
- "Horny-handed sons of toil" = manual laborers
- **Predominated** = majority
- But not exclusive

**Other Classes Identified**:
1. **Manufacturers** - Factory owners, industrialists
2. **Commercial travellers** - Sales representatives, traveling businessmen
3. **Clerks** - Office workers, white-collar employees
4. **Publicans** - Pub owners, licensed victuallers
5. **Publicans' wives** - Women attending, family involvement

**Significance**:
- Cross-class appeal of football
- But working-class majority
- Women present (publicans' wives)
- Shows football as family/community event

### The Reporter

**Possibly Same Person** as Pantomimists reporter (1886):
- **Similar phrase**: "horny-handed sons of toil"
- Five years apart (1886 and 1890)
- Same documentary style
- Same awareness of class composition
- Same sympathetic tone toward working class

**Shows**:
- Consistency in Sheffield press coverage
- Long-term documentation of working-class leisure
- Reporter aware of Saint Monday tradition
- Documenting social phenomenon over years

### Saint Monday in 1890

**Even by 1890**:
- Practice of Saint Monday **still observed in Sheffield**
- **Despite motivation of "bull week"** (economic incentive to work)
- 10,000+ willing to forgo wages for football
- Shows deep cultural embedding of practice

**Longevity of Saint Monday in Sheffield**:
- **1874**: E.P. Thompson notes it as "settled habit and custom"
- **1876-1886**: Data shows extensive midweek football (413 matches)
- **1890**: Still strong enough to draw 10,000+ on Monday in bull week
- **Persisted longer in Sheffield than elsewhere**

**Significance**:
- Saint Monday not economic opportunism
- Cultural practice embedded in identity

## Conclusions from the Data

### Saint Monday Evidence

**Overwhelming Pattern**:
- 294 of 413 midweek matches on Monday (71.2%)
- 13 of 18 high-attendance matches on Monday (72.2%)
- Consistent across all 10 seasons surveyed
- Even in 1890 during "bull week": 10,000+ attendance

**Thompson's 1874 Observation Confirmed**:
> "In Sheffield, where the cutlers had for centuries tenaciously honoured the Saint [Monday], it had become 'a settled habit and custom' which the steel mills themselves honoured"

**Data Shows**:
- Football provided means to maintain tradition
- Working-class used football to claim Monday leisure
- Employers either tolerating or unable to prevent
- Large steel mills forced to treat Monday as repair day
- Football embedded in workshop culture

### Saturday Half-Holiday Did Not Eliminate Monday

**Douglas A. Reid's Finding**:
- Saturday half-holiday appeared in Sheffield in 1840s
- Leisure emphasis should have shifted from Monday to Saturday

**Data Shows**:
- Workers gained Saturday leisure
- **But did not give up Monday**
- Both days became leisure time
- Workers expanded rather than shifted leisure
- Challenge to employer control increased

### Class Patterns in Midweek Football

**Working-Class Monday**:
- 71.2% of matches
- "Horny-handed sons of toil predominated"
- Senior clubs (Wednesday, Heeley) drew largest crowds
- Saint Monday tradition

**Middle-Class Thursday**:
- Thursday Wanderers: middle-class professionals
- Steel industry connections
- Well-paid, secure employment
- Different day but still midweek
- Shows multiple classes challenging work week

**Wednesday: Schools and Mixed**:
- Educational institutions (grammar schools, colleges)
- But also non-school clubs
- Different pattern than Monday or Thursday

### Financial Inducements Question

**Evidence of Payment**:
1. **Sheffield Zulus** (1879-1882): Confirmed profit-splitting
2. **Wednesday and Heeley**: Speculation about gate receipts (no evidence)
3. **Other clubs**: No evidence found

**Gate Receipts**:
- Few newspaper indications whether charges levied
- High attendances suggest money was made
- Where did it go?
- Player payment vs club funds unclear

**Second Rank Clubs**:
- **Unlikely remunerated**
- Simply taking advantage of Saint Monday
- Pursuing favourite leisure activity
- None appear in high-attendance matches

**Factory Teams**:
- Lockwood Brothers, Joseph Rodgers: No evidence of player payment
- But employers blessed midweek matches
- Worker morale benefit to firm
- Early works sport

### Professionalism Evolution

**Timeline**:
1. **1879-1882**: Sheffield Zulus split profits (early professionalism?)
2. **1876-1886**: Speculation about gate receipts at senior clubs
3. **1886-87**: Lockwood Brothers sign Wednesday players
4. **1887**: Wednesday forced to turn professional
5. **1885**: FA legalizes professionalism (after Sheffield evidence)

**Sheffield's Role**:
- Clear evidence of professionalism before FA approval
- Zulus showed players would take money
- Lockwood Brothers showed factory workers could compete
- Wednesday showed professionalism necessary for success
- Sheffield pressured national body to accept reality

### Football as Contested Social Space

**Workers Claiming Time**:
- 413 midweek matches (1876-86)
- 294 on Monday
- 20,000 at Pantomimists charity match
- 10,000+ at 1890 derby during bull week
- **Leisure taking precedence over work**

**Employers Responding**:
- Steel mills treated Monday as repair day (Thompson)
- Factory owners blessed works teams
- Some tolerance, some accommodation
- Unable to fully control worker leisure

**Football as Vehicle**:
- Provided legitimate reason for leisure
- Organized structure (fixtures, cups, trials)
- Community participation (spectating)
- Cross-class appeal (charity matches)
- Cultural practice embedded in identity
